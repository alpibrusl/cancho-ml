Benchmarks

What was timed, on what, and under what load. None of it is a claim yet.

Read this first. Every table on this page was taken on one Mac (arm64, 16 cores, macOS 26.2; the documents that name the chip say Apple M4 Max) that other users and other agents’ builds and benchmarks were also using. The load average (1 minute) was 2.4 or more in every cell, and up to 10 in the linear-algebra table: every cell is flagged load >= 2, and none is a quiet-machine number. A serial rerun on a quiet machine is pending. Until it is done, these are the conditions of one stretch of time, not measurements to quote.

The command line has not been timed. All of this is the libraries under ml; since stage W1 the verbs call the linear and logistic models, the pipeline and the metrics, and none of that was timed through the command line. There is no timing of ml fit, predict or eval in this repository.

Models against numpy and scikit-learn

Fit only, one thread, all n rows, 20 features, minimum of 7 interleaved samples. Cancho’s spread (maximum over minimum of the 7 samples) is 1.02 to 1.05. The 5- and 15-minute load averages printed with the cells were 6.6 to 7.3. What the table says. Cancho is slower than numpy’s same algorithm on logistic and linear regression (1.4 to 2.2 times) and slower than scikit-learn’s default logistic call (1.9 to 2.2 times); it is level with scikit-learn on k-means. Against scikit-learn’s LinearRegression it is 4.6 to 5.7 times faster, but that solver is an SVD-based least squares with centring, a slower algorithm: numpy’s same-algorithm column is the fair one. The linear-regression gap to numpy follows from the syrk kernel being 3.4 times behind dsyrk at 100,000 by 20 (below). Logistic regression runs 6 Newton passes; scikit-learn’s default needs 6 iterations and 7 or 8 evaluations, each of which reads X twice.

The first run of the million-row k-means printed 515 ms at one thread; the cause was not found, the rerun above agrees with the 306 to 320 ms of every other measurement of that cell, and the first line is kept in the raw log. Every thread owns a copy of its rows, made at every pass, because cancho cannot hand a thread a shared read-only slice (docs/linalg.md section 5.6, U2); the copy is in the time, which is why the speed-up is far from linear and linear regression, one pass, hardly gains. For the linear-algebra kernels, dot, gemv_t and gemv_n get slower with threads (0.38 to 0.65 of the one-thread speed) for the same reason; syrk at 100,000 by 64 takes 17.5 ms on one thread and 3.75 ms on sixteen, and gemm at 512 takes 10.8 ms and 2.25 ms (docs/bench/threads.txt, load 4.2, minimum of 5).

The verdict: 8 of the 29 cells match and 21 do not, by factors from 1.05 to 14.86. Square gemm is 13.8 to 16.5 times slower than Accelerate. Against the thinnest scipy call (dpotrf and dtrtrs through f2py, load 3.8 to 4.0, docs/bench/gate-lapack.jsonl) the solves still match and Cholesky does not at any size (1.09, 1.64 and 10.67 times at d = 21, 64 and 256): 6 of 29 cells match on the strictest reading. Numpy’s time on the gemv_n cell at 100,000 by 20 moved by 2.4 times between sessions with cancho’s unchanged, so the ratio of 1.06 on that cell should not be read either way. Why the misses are misses (found by ablation, docs/linalg.md section 5.3): the same algorithm in C, with no fused multiply-add, takes 0.95 to 1.16 times as long as cancho on dot, axpy, gemv, syrk and Cholesky at 256 columns, so the gap is the vendor library (hand-written kernels, the AMX unit) and the language, which has no SIMD vector type, and not cancho’s code generation. The fixed summation order costs the dot product 1.7 times: 200 to 250 us for the block rule against 140 us for 8 adjacent lanes, a decision for the maintainer that would change every dot-shaped reduction.

The log-loss is 3 times slower than a thin numpy version and 3 times faster than the scikit-learn call; which one a user compares with is theirs to say. The same terms summed by one left fold take 14.2 ms of the 16.0, so about 14 ns a row is the clip and cancho’s log; why the thin numpy version does the same work in 5 ms was not investigated. The fixed summation tree costs 1.13 times the left fold (16.00 against 14.20 ms) and has the order that does not depend on the thread count; the left fold is 413 ulp from the exact sum of the terms on this input and the tree 0.0 (docs/bench/metrics-sum-cost.txt, load 4.8 to 4.9, one input). The AUC sorts the two classes apart with a radix sort; why argsort is slower here was not investigated. The metrics have no threaded variant.

Forests

Classification, depth 8, 64 bins, 4 candidate features per node (mtry), bootstrap, 20 columns, train on the first 80% of the rows, test on the last 20%. Minimum of 7 interleaved rounds. Seconds. Source: docs/bench/tree_100000.txt and tree_1000000_b.txt, docs/models-tree.md section 10.9. Load average over the rounds: 3.3 to 4.9 (100,000 rows) and 2.6 to 5.0 (1,000,000 rows). Both are the lower-load session of two; the other session is in the document and the raw files.

These are not speed comparisons with scikit-learn. scikit-learn’s RandomForestClassifier is an exact-split forest: it looks for thresholds on the raw values. This library bins the values into quantiles first. The two make models of the same family by different algorithms, so the scikit-learn rows are the time to produce such a model, not the speed of the same algorithm, and no ratio is given. No histogram random forest was installed to compare with, and nothing was installed. scikit-learn ran with n_jobs = 1; that it ran on one thread was not checked with a counter. HistGradientBoosting with one iteration builds one tree with leaf-wise growth and second-order leaf values, so its row is context only.

Spreads (maximum over minimum) of the cancho cells are 1.01 to 1.12, except the 16-thread predictions (1.25 to 1.33), which are at or above the protocol’s limit of 1.25; the cells above it were not rerun. Scaling. Fit speed-up at 16 threads, against the same configuration on one thread, for 32 trees: 6.61 times at 100,000 rows and 3.38 at 1,000,000; predict 9.28 and 10.58 times. The 10-tree fits do not divide by 4, 8 or 16 and use at most 10 threads. The explanation, by elimination and not measured: the parent builds a copy of the bin matrix and the labels for each thread, one after another, because a thread cannot be given a shared read-only slice. At 16 threads on a machine with 12 performance cores and a load of 3 or more, read the cells as “no worse than”. The forest’s bytes are identical at every thread count. Quality is not the same as speed: the classification forests agree with scikit-learn’s to 0.001 AUC, and the regression forest does not (the red gate).

On the float file, reading every cell in pass 1 is 13% slower than cancho-table’s own reader (17.1 against 19.6 M/s) and on short decimals it is level. The whole load is about 2.3 times a pass: it reads every cell twice by design, because the shape must be known before the matrix is allocated. Where the 13% goes was not profiled; a copy of each record into a buffer before splitting is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

What was not measured

  • The command line. ml fit, predict and eval have no benchmark (the constant model needs no matrix; the pipeline models read one).
  • A quiet machine. The serial rerun is pending, and so are the quiet-machine versions of every table above.
  • Another machine. Everything is one Mac, arm64. A Linux x86-64 box has not been used.
  • Memory. No gate measures it. The reader’s refusal of a 200 MB file peaked at 1.5 MB, and the tree fit holds about 16 times 35 MB of thread copies at 16 threads for 800,000 rows (docs/models-tree.md section 10.9); there is no measurement of a model fit.
  • Rows above a million for the linear models, and 10,000,000 for anything. The tree fit is limited to 220 rows in the library.
  • How to rerun: python3 scripts/bench_models.py, bench_models_threads.py, bench_linalg.py --gate --c, bench_metrics.py, bench_tree.py and bench_reader.py. They were not run for this page.
