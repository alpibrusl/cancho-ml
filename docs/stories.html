1 · The number to beat

start herefit --model constanteval

“I rent bicycles. Can a model predict how many I will rent tomorrow? Before I believe one, what does not knowing anything score?” — Pilar

Every model is judged against the dumbest one: always answer the average of the training days. ml fit --model constant is exactly that, and ml eval scores it on days it has never seen (the last 60 of 240; the first 180 train). The model is a file; the score is a document.

a real run
$ ml fit --model constant --target rentals --output base.ml rent_train.csv
{
  "model": "constant",
  "task": "regression",
  "rows": 180,
  "columns": 5,
  "target": "rentals",
  "sha256": "57008c9388baa886b106a43586680dd8c783315474d7a5640c68d7242eb39de0",
  "written": true,
  "summary": {"mean": "90.455556"}
}
a real run
$ ml eval --model-file base.ml rent_test.csv
{
  "model": "constant",
  "task": "regression",
  "rows": 60,
  "on": "other",
  "metrics": {
    "rmse": "31.883749",

Reading it. The mean of the 180 training days is 90.455556 rentals, and answering it every day is wrong by 31.88 bicycles on a typical held-out day (the root-mean-square error). That is the line a real model has to get under, in the same units, on the same days.

2 · A line through the days

a first real modelfit --model linearpredicteval

“Warm, dry weekdays are busy; wet holidays are not. Can it learn that?”

A linear model finds one weight per column. The fit prints them, with the intercept; ml predict applies the stored model to the held-out days and ml eval scores it. The arithmetic of the fit is a normal equation solved exactly where a sum decides the result, and the file records the SHA-256 of what it learnt.

a real run

3 · Empty cells are a decision

a plan, a refusal, a repairml.input.missing-value--step impute;scale

“Eleven days the humidity sensor was down. The cells are empty.”

A model cannot guess a missing number, and neither does ml: the fit is refused with a rule, a hint and the command that repairs it. Then the plan says more: impute the humidity with the mean (of the training rows, stored in the model file, so that predict fills gaps with the same number) and standardise two columns. Every step is fitted on the training rows and stored with the estimator in one file; there is no way to predict with a different transformation.

a real run
$ ml fit --model linear --target rentals --output gap.ml rent_gaps.csv
exit 8

  "hint": "add an impute step for the columns, for example impute column=a,b strategy=mean.",
  "detail": {"columns": 1, "names": "humidity"},
  "repair": {
    "kind": "retry",

4 · Will they come back?

a yes/no questionfit --model logisticpredict --probaeval

“I mailed a coupon to some of my customers. Who is likely to come back, and how sure can I be?”

Logistic regression answers yes or no with a probability. The fit is Newton’s method on the exact log-loss; predict --proba gives the label and its probability for each customer the model has not seen, and eval scores them: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, the area under the ROC curve and the log-loss.

a real run

  ]
}

5 · When a line is not enough

non-linear models--model tree--model forest

“The points inside the circle are one class. Logistic regression gives me a straight line. What now?”

6 · Do not trust one split

cross-validationcv --folds 5 --repeats 2

“The tree scored well on my test points. Was I lucky with which points I held out? And how deep should it be?”

ml cv fits the model on every training fold and scores it on the fold held out, K folds and R repeats, and writes no file. For each depth of the tree, five folds twice, with a seed: ten scores per depth.

a real run
$ ml cv --model tree --task classification --max-depth 4 --target inside --folds 5 \
    --repeats 2 --seed 3 ring_train.csv
{
  "strategy": "kfold",
  "folds": 5,
  "repeats": 2,
  "results": [
    {
      "repeat": 0,
      "fold": 0,
      "train": 240,
      "test": 60,
      "metrics": {
        "accuracy": "0.8833333333333333",
        "precision": "0.7407407407407407",
        "recall": "1.0",
        "f1": "0.851063829787234",
        "roc_auc": "0.9125",
        "log_loss": "0.30923418223921345"
      }
    },
    "..."
  ]
}
Reading it. A tree of depth 1 scores 0.653 on average, depth 2 0.723, depth 4 the best, 0.942. The dots are the ten folds of each depth: the spread tells how much one held-out set can mislead. A single test split gives one dot; the cross-validation gives ten, and the same seed gives the same folds on any machine.

7 · Same model, any machine

reproducibilityfit --trackruns verify--threads

“My colleague refit it on the server with sixteen cores. Is it the same model?”

A forest is a bag of random trees, which is the usual way to get a result nobody can reproduce. Here every tree has its own random stream derived from the seed and the tree’s index, so the number of threads cannot change a tree. fit --track DIR writes a run record: canonical JSON, named by the hash of the inputs that determine the model, with no timestamp and no host name. runs verify refits from the record, in memory, and compares the SHA-256 of the model file.

a real run
$ ml fit --model forest --task classification --trees 40 --max-depth 6 --seed 2 \

10 · Two kinds of pulse

physics: clusters with no labelsfit --model kmeanspredicteval

“The detector records the height and the rise time of every pulse. I think there are two kinds of particle. Without labels, can the data say so?”

k-means has no target: fit needs none and refuses one. The columns are standardised first (a pulse height in the tens and a rise time in the tens of nanoseconds have different units), --k 2 asks for two centres, and the initial centres are drawn from --seed, so the clusters are the same on every run.

a real run
$ ml fit --model kmeans --k 2 --seed 1 --step 'scale column=* kind=standard' --output \
    km.ml pulses.csv
{"model": "kmeans", "rows": 240, "target": null}
a real run
$ ml eval --model-file km.ml pulses.csv
{
  "clusters": 2,
  "metrics": {
    "inertia": "82.78133125890389",
    "sizes": [97, 143],
    "mean_distance": ["0.602540773533933", "0.4611493301696197"]
  }
}
Reading it. Two clusters of 97 and 143 pulses. The generator kept the true kind of each pulse (the file does not have it): the clusters agree with it on 1 of the pulses. With real data there is no such check, and the honest statement is the one the tool prints: the inertia, the sizes and the mean distance of each cluster, not that these are two particles.

12 · Who does the ad actually move?

marketing: uplift, two models and a randomized testfit --model logisticpredict --proba

“The ad lifts sales by about one point overall. Is it one point for everybody, or a lot for some and nothing — or worse — for others?” — the same head of growth, a month later

Because the ad was shown to a random half of the customers (the stories of cancho-stats showed why that matters), the customers who saw it and those who did not are comparable in every respect, and so is a model fitted to each. The simplest way to ask who is moved is to fit two models, one on the customers shown the ad and one on the held-out customers, and to subtract: for a new customer, the predicted chance of buying with the ad minus the predicted chance without. That difference is the predicted uplift. Two fits, one per file:

a real run
$ ml fit --model logistic --target bought --output u_holdout.ml \
    uplift_holdout_train.csv
{
  "model": "logistic",
  "rows": 7019,
  "coefficients": [
    {"name": "recency_days", "value": "-0.003622167772505198"},
    {"name": "orders_12m", "value": "0.27040238679337175"}
  ]
}
a real run
$ ml fit --model logistic --target bought --output u_ads.ml uplift_ads_train.csv
{
  "model": "logistic",
  "rows": 6981,
  "coefficients": [
    {"name": "recency_days", "value": "-0.0007877475094626317"},
    {"name": "orders_12m", "value": "0.21614628475463352"}
  ]
}

Then predict --proba with each model on a second, later group of 14000 customers, who were also randomly split, and the uplift of each is the difference of the two probabilities. A prediction is only a claim until it is checked, and here it can be checked against the truth: sort the new customers from the highest predicted uplift (the top fifth) to the lowest (the bottom fifth), cut them into five equal groups, and in each group compare the buyers among those who were shown the ad with those who were not.

Reading it. Over all 14000 test customers the ad lifts conversion by +1.1 points. But in the fifth of customers that the models picked as the most movable it lifts it by +2.2 points, and in the fifth picked as the least movable by -1.4: for them the ad does nothing, or slightly harms. The models learned that from one number per customer, how long ago they last bought and how often they buy. The groups hold 2800 customers each, so each bar carries a margin of about a point or two (the middle ones are not reliably ordered), and that is how a model like this is to be read: the ends, not the middle. The models are linear in the log odds; a forest per arm (--model forest) is the usual next step, and the same test on randomized data is how to know it helped.

13 · Spend the budget on the right third

“We can only afford to contact a third of them. Take the customers most likely to buy, right?” — the finance partner, who wants a number

There are two natural rankings. Most likely to buy uses the model of the customers shown the ad, and puts the loyal first. Most movable uses the uplift of the previous story. The test group was randomized, so a ranking can be scored honestly: contact the top q percent by that ranking, and the buyers the contact adds are the difference in conversion between those shown the ad and those not, times the size of the group. This is a gain curve: how many extra buyers each plan buys as the contacted share grows from zero to everyone.

Reading it. Contacting everybody buys 147 extra buyers in the 14000 customers of the test. Contacting the top 30% by chance to buy gets 24: not much more than the 44 that contacting 30% at random would. The same 30% chosen by uplift gets 90, and the uplift curve peaks at 204 extra buyers when 90% are contacted, above the total of contacting everyone: the last customers on the list lose the shop sales when they are contacted, and a plan that leaves them out does better than a plan that does not. The curves are estimates on one random split (the stats stories explain how wide such estimates are, and a bootstrap of the gain is one way to put an interval on them), so the ordering is clear and the exact peak is not. ml fitted the models and made the predictions; the arithmetic of the curve is the page’s, from the printed probabilities and the arm and outcome of each test customer.
